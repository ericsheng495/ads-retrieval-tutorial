How the two stages fit together

Read recommenders from zero first. This lesson answers the questions in the discussion you shared: how models, embeddings, vector search, features, and training connect to one recommendation request.

1. Two different computational jobs

Imagine Mira opens an app with a million possible items. In our invented design, retrieval returns 500 candidates. A heavier ranker scores those 500 before final selection. Retrieval asks “which items should we consider?” Ranking asks “given those items and richer information, which should come first or have most value?”

This split is a computation strategy. With a small catalog, one model can score everything. Large systems may have additional cheap-ranking, heavy-ranking, and re-ranking steps. “Two-stage” is a useful abstraction, not a universal count of production services. A retrieval similarity and a ranker's event probability can also have different meanings; they need not share a score scale.

2. What happens before a two-tower retrieval request?

A two-tower model computes u = user_encoder(user, context) and v = item_encoder(item_features), with score dot(u,v).

Before serving, run the item encoder over the catalog and store item vectors with item IDs. Build an ANN index over those vectors. At request time, compute Mira's query vector once and search the index. You do not need to run the item tower again for every possible user/item pair.

Exact search can calculate every catalog dot product. Approximate nearest-neighbor search examines promising regions or graph paths, accepting possible missed exact neighbors to reduce work. A vector database can host this search; a library such as FAISS is another component choice. Neither automatically learns the query representation or chooses what outcome to optimize.

3. Do candidates come from every possible item?

Define the logical search catalog first. Inventory, locale, access, targeting, or other eligibility rules can limit it. Partitions, filter-aware search, or overfetch followed by filtering are possible implementations. Mutable rules may need rechecking near display time.

Searching a catalog does not mean running an expensive joint neural model for every pair. An index was prepared earlier; the request queries that structure. Several retrieval sources can also contribute: popularity, similar-to-recently-viewed items, content matches, and personalized embeddings. Merge IDs, deduplicate, track sources, and check eligibility before expensive scoring.

“Whole-catalog search” describes intended coverage even when ANN does not inspect every item. State the actual candidate universe when comparing recall: a narrower catalog changes the evaluation task.

4. Why two towers fit retrieval so well

The key property is separability. Item encoding is independent of the current query, and the final score supports efficient vector search. Each tower may be deep and nonlinear within its own features. The outputs interact through the similarity operation.

Saying “two towers have no interactions” is too strong: the dot product is a compatibility interaction and the learned encoders can represent rich patterns. The restriction is that an arbitrary joint user/item network cannot be inserted before caching one item vector for every query.

For example, “how many times this user saw this exact ad in the last hour” is a pair-specific feature. A ranker can use it directly. Making an item's cached vector depend on it would require different item vectors for different users, breaking that simple item-side cache. More elaborate retrieval designs exist; our basic two-tower reference does not implement them.

5. What a DLRM-style ranker does differently

A standard DLRM-style path transforms numeric features, looks up categorical embeddings, computes feature interactions, and passes them through a top network. The final joint score is generally not a single independent query/item dot product. See the original DLRM paper.

Its relevant scoring computation runs for each candidate pair, possibly batched. That is easier to afford for 500 pairs than a million. DLRM is one option; an MLP, Wide & Deep, DCN, or another suitable model can also fill the role. Choose with data, prediction quality, and serving cost, not a universal architecture rule.

Internal embedding lookups or dot products do not make a model's final score ANN-searchable. Ask whether that final function factors into independently computed representations under a supported search similarity.

6. Can either model be used in both stages?

A two-tower model can rank a shortlist. Sorting its scores already produces an ordering. A second-stage two-tower can use larger encoders, fresher features, or another objective. Measure whether it improves on the first-stage score.

A joint ranker can score a full catalog when the budget permits. Small catalogs, offline jobs, or optimized special cases may allow this. At large online scale, a nonseparable score is not automatically an ordinary ANN retrieval function. Another retrieval mechanism, approximation, distillation, or architecture may be needed. Some factorization variants have search-friendly decompositions; do not assign every model in a family to one mandatory stage.

Question Two-tower dot-product score General joint DLRM-style score
Can it rank supplied candidates? Yes Yes
Can independently cached vectors represent its final score? Yes under the encoder contract Not generally
Can ordinary ANN directly search its final score? Yes with compatible similarity/index Generally needs additional structure or approximation
Can it score every item? Yes by exact vector scoring In principle yes; computation may be prohibitive

Two-tower retrieval plus a joint ranker is a coherent design, not proof that it is the best combination for every product.

7. Are collaborative and content filtering separate stages?

No. These terms describe information and learning approaches, not locations in the serving funnel. Collaborative patterns come from interactions across people and items. Content information comes from descriptions, metadata, or media. Either stage can combine both.

An ID-only two-tower trained on interaction pairs is collaborative in spirit. Adding genre, text, image, or history features can make it hybrid. A ranker can likewise use IDs, learned collaborative representations, and content. Do not automatically label retrieval “collaborative” and ranking “content.”

8. Does ranking reuse the retrieval embeddings?

It can, but does not have to. Options include frozen retrieval vectors or scores as ranker features; separate ranker embedding tables; pretrained initialization with a ranker-specific fine-tuned copy; or deliberately shared parameters with a coordinated training/export process.

The ranker may also use richer original inputs: request context, metadata, pair-specific exposure history, retrieval source, and other query-time signals. Each feature needs a consistent training/serving definition.

If ranking fine-tunes weights also used by retrieval, cached item vectors can become incompatible with the new query encoder. Keep separate copies or update dependent serving artifacts together. Equal dimensions do not establish that two embedding spaces are compatible.

9. Can pretrained video embeddings generate candidates?

Yes. Pretrained representations can supply a content-retrieval source or item-tower features. You still need a compatible query representation and an objective aligned with recommendation. A database of video vectors alone does not tell the system which vector to search for Mira.

One experiment pools her previous item vectors in the same space. Another trains an aligned query encoder or projects pretrained features through a learned item tower. Do not compare arbitrary user and video models' vectors merely because their dimensions match.

Semantic similarity is not the same as behavioral value. Two videos can share a topic while one is a poor choice now. Compare against popularity and interaction-trained retrieval using chronological evaluation and cold-item coverage, rather than assuming a pretrained encoder solves personalization.

10. Trace training, serving, and feedback

In a teaching system, train the retriever on positive interactions with a documented sampling mechanism. Export compatible item vectors and mappings. Separately train a ranker with suitable labeled examples and a defined loss. Observed clicks only cover displayed items; the training distribution must be considered relative to the candidates and exposures seen during serving.

At serving time: fetch context → retrieve → merge/filter → construct ranker features → batch-score → select → log item IDs, exposure, versions, and eventual outcomes. Models can update on different schedules while their feature and artifact contracts remain coherent.

Measure retrieval coverage and cost, ranking quality and calibration where appropriate, then end-to-end outcomes. Ranking cannot recover an item absent from its shortlist. A retrieval change also changes the ranker's input distribution, so isolated model results do not settle full-system performance.

11. Build one connected miniature

Use the MovieLens retriever to produce 100 unseen movies per user. Attach metadata and an explicitly defined extra score to those candidates. Keep candidate IDs fixed when comparing ranking policies; separately vary retrieval's budget to see what downstream coverage changes.

Use synthetic impression labels for a toy logistic ranker or a suitable real impression dataset for CTR work. MovieLens ratings are not ad impressions. Write down which conclusions your example can and cannot establish.

Meta's Instagram Explore case study illustrates several retrieval sources, caching, and multiple ranking stages. It is a dated production example, not a requirement to copy its design.
