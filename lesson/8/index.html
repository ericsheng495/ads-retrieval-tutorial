Production & transfer

Concept

Offline retrieval estimates performance on a chosen set of observed labels. Online behavior also depends on exposure, ranking, auctions, fresh inventory, and user response. A randomized experiment tests a change under those conditions, subject to its randomization and measurement assumptions.

Transferring to another dataset tests a different boundary: whether the pipeline's data contract and model assumptions fit a new task. Reusing code does not require pretending query identity and evaluation are unchanged.

Offline improvement is a product hypothesis

Logged data reflects an earlier policy. A new retriever changes candidates, downstream selections, and future feedback. A larger historical metric does not by itself establish the net effect of all these changes.

In an A/B experiment, treatment is the changed policy and control is its comparison. Randomization helps make groups comparable, but interpretation depends on assumptions and uncertainty. Guardrails are metrics that must remain acceptable even when the primary metric improves, such as error rate or latency.

Shared budgets or inventory can couple the groups. Read the two-sided design and budget-split lessons to see why choosing a traffic percentage is not the whole experimental design. Define the full-rollout effect you want before interpreting a mixed-market group difference as that effect.

Worked example

Retriever B improves offline target recall from 0.25 to 0.28 but increases p95 latency from 20 to 45 ms. More candidates survive, yet serving timeouts or weaker downstream outcomes could erase the benefit. Decide in advance which success metric and latency guardrail would support shipping.

A conversion may arrive two days after its impression. An impression from an hour ago without a conversion is not necessarily a settled negative. Labels need an observation window and delayed joins keyed to the correct impression. Separately, features must reflect information available before the prediction, even if label construction uses later outcomes.

For transfer, a new OTTO session has no trained user-ID row. Its prefix history must construct the query vector. A MovieLens ID-only implementation cannot run unchanged on that query without defining a fallback or adapting the encoder.

Application to this project

You will write an experiment plan rather than deploy to real traffic. Then use one transfer dataset to stress the design. OTTO provides an official data/evaluation repository; H&M has a separate competition task. Internal candidate Recall@K and official competition scores must be labeled separately.

Implement it

  1. Write a one-page hypothesis with randomization unit, primary metric, guardrails, power/duration logic, delayed-label window, and rollback criteria.
  2. Sketch event schemas: impression ID, user/session, item ID, event/prediction time, model/index version, eligibility context, and eventual outcome.
  3. Specify a label join that avoids duplicated conversions and incomplete windows.
  4. Build a small OTTO or H&M adapter with query histories, targets, catalog, and time cutoffs.
  5. Run popularity plus a suitable learned baseline; preserve dataset-specific split rules.
  6. Report at least two failure modes that differ from MovieLens and explain required adaptations.

Read Schnabel for the logged-feedback problem and revisit the Snap post for the ML development cycle. Use OTTO's official repository for task definitions; consult H&M's evaluation page before reporting a competition score.

Self-check

  • Which assumption connects a logged offline metric to an online claim?
  • What makes a label delayed rather than a feature leaked from the future?
  • What would you measure before recommending this model to your future team?
